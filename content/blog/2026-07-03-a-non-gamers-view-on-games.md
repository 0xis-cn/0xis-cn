> [For folk literature,] you need folk allusions — if cowardly, say Vuu Dhahlang; if brave, say old Huang Dyung.  
> — Lǎo Shě, *The Agony of Creating Popular Literature*

I am the kind of person who emphasizes at the beginning of each smalltalk that I am a non-gamer. Unfortunately, this statement requires clarification. I open Tetris from time to time, and occasionally linger on [neal.fun](https://neal.fun) and Lichess, but *Doom* is beyond choice. I always make the claim abruptly, regardless of how others would interpret it, maybe contrary to my intention. I envy gamers, in fact. I envy them like the hypothetical narrator in a clichéd advertisement envies those who read. Their focus on games and the clear separation between games and work are beyond my imagination.

<!--more-->

Since I have not had time to examine the concepts, I rephrase it as follows: <ml-h>I do not play things that are generally classified as games that are prevalently played with digital devices exclusively and need non-trivial GUI operations.</ml-h>

Intrinsically, the idea to develop a game other than a board game simulator never emerged in mine, since I have no idea what this actually is. Thirteen years after [Draengyu](https://poisson.laerhsif.com/2013-08-07/) and [Yin Wang](https://yinwang.org/posts/braid), I squeeze my words on games, to expose, extrapolate, or perform my repellence.

### The last fall of Icarus

Is it the pattern of dungeons and levels that shapes the skills of game characters, or vice versa? From a teleological perspective, it seems the former shapes the latter, given that game characters are designed to flow within the reward cycle. However, characters share a deeper root, early as troubadours (even the one-size-fits-all trope of online games, the installable, interchangible, cost-driven characters, would trace back to Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*) and extending to Marvel series which make a show of cobbling together superpowers, always sacrificing narrative coherence to create an all-star lineup. In contrast, maps are often products of rationalization, emerging after pedants interrogate minstrels — a phenomenon visible throughout the formation of classical novels (even in reality, royal courts or commander’s mansions with numerous conflicting reports are doing some fiction). Before narration, sunken warships become divine statues, the million soldiers’ footprints blurred, only clashes of brave generals emerging; after narration, awkwardly frequent roads are then questioned. Before narration, the monkey stood firm with his magic cudgel; after narration, people delineate from memory a reasonable journey, connecting the Flowing Sand River and the Little Western Heaven — just as game developers have done once again.

Therefore, I dare to say that games’ exploration of temporal and spatial attributes is still quite rudimentary. I suspect that a seasoned gamer would often feel as cramped by *Dota* or *LOL* as by *3D Pinball: Space Cadet* — the entire world is nothing more than a lavish table. Maybe one under a disturbed gravitational field and shrouded in fog. After all, even *Miegakure* has already far exceeded human imagination.

The brief appearance of Icarus, who owns no morph in *Metamorphoses*, makes it hard not to sigh after closing the book. It, condemned to change the laws of nature, becomes the beginning of a hidden thread (perhaps buried under the sea like Icarus himself): transforming into the young nameless detective, the conscripted Sakura Kinomoto, the soaring Lynae. But this time, the singularity of extraordinary abilities after extraordinary beings is also co-opted by gameness. No matter how lifelike cosplayers are, characters no longer restore the glory of classical novels. <ml-h>The wings that challenged the gravitational field have become a new gravitational field; the innocent-looking girl, rebranded as a transportation character, turned out to be a node of endless gravity, calculation, and optimal configuration.</ml-h> Thus, the teleological explanation still prevails.

### Magic is destiny

Technically inseparable character illustrations provide a home for loyal magic. Since Diablo, heads-up displays have finally complemented these stately and elegant corpses, becoming a modern part of them, just like daggars under a dance costume.

In Wang Yi-Chia’s [depiction](https://ycwang-world.com/e0208/) of *Investiture of the Gods*, behind the miraculous magic lies a somber fate. That differs Juliet’s forty-two-hour narcosis from a science fiction. The extraordinary beings endowed with prophetic power tacitly understand fate, even their own downfall.

Gamers have existed for too long — in the magazines I obtained from my brother, the featured cool boy talking about *StarCraft* and *Warcraft* on a skateboard, making vomiting gestures at these boring adults taking cameras in hand who only knew *Bubble Bobble*, is now in his thirties, as *Dota*-obsessed frustrated engineer population — so that transient fate has been replaced by people’s own bleak fate (and that of generations of games serving them). Just as Julian wrote in his game-ending poem, the short dream of a game is followed by the long dream of life. Flitting here and there, like the Babylonian lottery or rotating card art, euphoria and rage fade into the suddenly extinguished surroundings, unable to safeguard what we love in the endless river of time — our zeitgeist.

O soaring Lynae, who lives as someone else, my heart is by your side.
